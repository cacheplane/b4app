# Rescale the iPhone app to HIG metrics

The iOS 26 HIG makes a small set of firm numerical demands, and b4app's iOS companion currently misses most of them. **Body text defaults to 17 pt with an 11 pt floor "for both custom and system fonts"** ([HIG Typography](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/typography)). Controls get a **44x44 pt hit region (28x28 pt absolute minimum)** ([HIG Accessibility](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/accessibility)). iPhone layout margins are **16–20 pt from the system, never hard-coded** ([ContentMarginGuide.container](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/contentmarginguide/container)). Text should be enlargeable to **at least 200 percent**, and layouts should reflow rather than truncate at accessibility sizes ([HIG Accessibility](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/accessibility)). Nearly everything else is qualitative and comes down to one rule: use the standard components, which in iOS 26 means Liquid Glass toolbars, taller list rows, bottom-placed search, and system menus, and don't hard-code their metrics ([Adopting Liquid Glass](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/technologyoverviews/adopting-liquid-glass)). The shared SwiftUI was drawn at a desktop scale: 15 pt body, 13 pt rows, about 28 pt rows, 24 pt margins, fixed-size marks, a duplicated title, and a Mac keyboard hint. The fix is mostly mechanical. Give the iOS build its own type scale anchored at the HIG Large sizes, scaling with `Font.custom(_:size:relativeTo:)`. Let `List` and the system margins size the rows and gutters. Scale icons with `@ScaledMetric`. Keep a single navigation title. Attach the composer with `safeAreaBar`. The owner's D-053 sidebar structure can stay, but rows must clear 44 pt and the profile button must sit in the bottom safe area. Moving New thread, Search, and filter into toolbars is the layout Apple's own samples use, and it is the natural follow-up.

## Seventeen points is the floor for body text, and custom fonts must scale themselves

Apple defines eleven text styles. Each one is a size, a leading, and a weight at every Dynamic Type setting. Every style is Regular except Headline (Semibold). The emphasized (bold) variant is Bold for Large Title through Title 2 and Semibold for the rest ([HIG Typography](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/typography)). The iOS table below gives size/leading in points. The standard range runs xSmall to xxxLarge, and AX1–AX5 appear only when Larger Accessibility Sizes is on ([WWDC24 Get started with Dynamic Type](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2024/10074/)). A program that called `UIFont.preferredFont(forTextStyle:compatibleWith:)` on an iOS 26.5 simulator during this research returned exactly these point sizes for all 132 cells, so the table is current.

Two features of the table shape the design. First, small styles grow far more than large ones. Caption 2 grows about 3.6x by AX5 and Large Title only 1.76x, so at AX5 a 60 pt title sits barely above 53 pt body. Weight and color have to carry hierarchy at the top end. Second, the HIG minimums apply to custom faces. The guidance is **"default 17 pt, minimum 11 pt" for both custom and system fonts**, prefer Regular through Bold, and avoid Ultralight, Thin, and Light ([HIG Typography](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/typography)). The page also says to "minimize the number of typefaces you use", which two families (Hanken Grotesk for UI, JetBrains Mono for code) already satisfy.

### Custom fonts get Dynamic Type only through `relativeTo:`, and not on the system's curve

The HIG puts responsibility for custom fonts on the app: "If you use a custom font, make sure it implements the same behaviors", meaning Dynamic Type and Bold Text ([HIG Typography](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/typography)). In SwiftUI there are three paths. `Font.custom(name, size:relativeTo:)` scales relative to a chosen style ([docs](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/font/custom(_:size:relativeto:))). `Font.custom(name, size:)` silently scales relative to `.body` ([docs](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/font/custom(_:size:))). `Font.custom(name, fixedSize:)` never scales ([docs](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/font/custom(_:fixedsize:))). `Font.system(size:)` is also fixed, so a fallback like `.system(size:weight:)` in `Sources/WorkspaceUI/Theme/LLAFonts.swift` drops Dynamic Type entirely if font registration fails. `.custom` already falls back to the system font *and keeps scaling* ([Applying custom fonts to text](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/applying-custom-fonts-to-text)), so the fallback branch is unnecessary.

Apple says only that scaled custom fonts grow "in a manner that's similar to the way the text style you supply is scaled" ([Scaling fonts automatically](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/uikit/scaling-fonts-automatically)). Measurements on the iOS 26.5 simulator show what "similar" means. The multiplier is the style's *leading* ratio, not its point-size ratio, rounded to whole points. **A 17 pt custom font relative to `.body` resolves to 22 pt at xxxLarge and 48 pt at AX5, against the system's 23 and 53.** Body is 15, 15, 16, 17, 19, 20, 22, 26, 31, 37, 43, 48 across the twelve sizes. A 13 pt font relative to `.footnote` reaches 38 at AX5. An 11 pt font relative to `.caption2` jumps from 11 to 15 at the first step above Large, the steepest single step of any style; Use Your Loaf saw the same anomaly ([Use Your Loaf](https://useyourloaf.com/blog/using-a-custom-font-with-dynamic-type/)). The behavior is undocumented and could change in a later release. Even so, 17 to 48 is 2.8x, which clears the 200 percent target. The practical rules: set every base size to the HIG Large value, pick the `relativeTo` style whose role matches, and avoid `.caption2` for anything that should stay small. SwiftUI has no `maximumPointSize`, so cap an individual view with `.dynamicTypeSize(...DynamicTypeSize.xxxLarge)`. Apple suggests pairing any clamp with `accessibilityShowsLargeContentViewer()` ([dynamicTypeSize(_:)](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/view/dynamictypesize(_:)-26aj0)).

Bold Text is the second behavior. SwiftUI exposes it as `EnvironmentValues.legibilityWeight` ([docs](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/environmentvalues/legibilityweight)), and it "doesn't synthesize bold or italic styling" for custom faces ([Applying custom fonts to text](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/applying-custom-fonts-to-text)). The bundle ships Hanken Grotesk in Regular and SemiBold only, so the font layer has to map Regular to SemiBold itself when `legibilityWeight == .bold`. The HIG gives no mapping table. The research also turned up a trap in the repo's own files: the default instance of `Sources/WorkspaceUI/Resources/Fonts/JetBrainsMono-Variable.ttf` is **JetBrainsMonoRoman-Thin**, the weight the HIG says to avoid. `LLAFonts.swift` correctly asks for the named `JetBrainsMono-Regular` instance, and that must not regress.

Apple gives no iOS rule for code size. Measured x-heights settle it optically. JetBrains Mono's x-height is 0.550 em and Hanken Grotesk's is 0.493 em, so **15 pt JetBrains Mono reads the same size as 17 pt Hanken body** (8.25 vs 8.38 pt x-height). Code should scale like body and should never truncate. The HIG's "avoid truncating text in scrollable regions" ([HIG Typography](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/typography)) points to wrapping or a horizontal `ScrollView` for long lines.

## Forty-four points and system margins replace hand-tuned density

Apple gives the touch target once on the Buttons page, "a hit region of at least 44x44 pt" ([HIG Buttons](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/buttons)), and again as a table in Accessibility: iOS default 44x44, minimum 28x28. The Accessibility page treats spacing as equally important, at **about 12 pt of padding around bezeled elements and about 24 pt around unbezeled ones** ([HIG Accessibility](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/accessibility)). The hit region is what counts, not the glyph. A 20 pt symbol in a `.frame(minWidth: 44, minHeight: 44)` with `.contentShape(Rectangle())` ([contentShape](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/view/contentshape(_:eofill:))) meets the rule, and two such boxes placed edge to edge leave the 24 pt of air around each bare icon. A forum report says image-only `Button` labels hit-test only at the image bounds ([Forums 849832](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/849832)), which is one more reason to set the content shape explicitly. The 28 pt minimum is for dense secondary controls, never the send button.

Lists have no published row height. `defaultMinListRowHeight` is an environment value with no documented default ([docs](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/environmentvalues/defaultminlistrowheight)), so the familiar "44 pt row" is legacy knowledge. What Apple does say for iOS 26 is that lists, tables, and forms "have a larger row height and padding", with rounder section corners ([Adopting Liquid Glass](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/technologyoverviews/adopting-liquid-glass)). It also says rows "may need to grow in height so that text isn't cropped" under Dynamic Type ([HIG Layout](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/layout)). Since iOS 26 honors `listRowInsets` more strictly than iOS 18 did ([listRowInsets](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/view/listrowinsets(_:))), the cleanest move is to stop overriding row height and insets on iOS and let the system decide. The Lists page itself was last changed in June 2023 ([HIG Lists and tables](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/lists-and-tables)). It asks for succinct row text, titles with detail in a drill-down, a persistent highlight on the selected navigation row, and swipe actions. Apple's own `swipeActions` example is a messages list with unread on the leading edge and delete or flag on the trailing edge ([swipeActions](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/view/swipeactions(edge:allowsfullswipe:content:))).

Margins follow the same pattern. Apple's API reference says iPhone system margins "range from 16 to 20 points" and tells developers to "apply or read the guide instead of writing these values into your layout" ([ContentMarginGuide.container](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/contentmarginguide/container)). That API is listed as iOS 27.1, but the margin range describes the system generally. UIKit raises any custom margin below `systemMinimumLayoutMargins` to the minimum ([docs](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/uikit/uiviewcontroller/systemminimumlayoutmargins)). No Apple page describes an 8 pt grid; that is a third-party convention. On iPad, single-column text should respect a readable width ([readableContentGuide](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/uikit/uiview/readablecontentguide)). SwiftUI approximates it with a `maxWidth` frame. The HIG defines the safe area as the region not covered by hardware such as the Dynamic Island or by bars, and calls respecting it "essential" ([HIG Layout](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/layout)). It also replaces solid bar backgrounds with a scroll edge effect. SwiftUI's iOS 26 `safeAreaBar(edge:)` is built for exactly that: it insets content like `safeAreaInset` and "extends the edge effect of any scroll views" ([safeAreaBar](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/view/safeareabar(edge:alignment:spacing:content:))). The September 2026 Layout revision removed the older iOS section ("Avoid full-width buttons…"), and now says "No additional considerations for iOS" ([HIG Layout](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/layout); [2025 revision](https://web.archive.org/web/20251201140322/https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/layout)). WWDC25 still recommends "a capsule with extra margin" near iPhone screen edges ([WWDC25 356](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/356/)).

The HIG covers composers only indirectly. Larger input belongs in a text view rather than a field. The field should be sized "to the quantity of anticipated text". The leading end indicates purpose and the trailing end offers features ([HIG Text fields](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/text-fields)). Controls above the keyboard should use Liquid Glass, the keyboard layout guide, and standard padding ([HIG Virtual keyboards](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/virtual-keyboards)). In SwiftUI that means `TextField(axis: .vertical)` with a line-limit range ([docs](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/textfield/init(_:text:axis:))), `.defaultScrollAnchor(.bottom)` on the transcript ([docs](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/view/defaultscrollanchor(_:))), and `.scrollDismissesKeyboard(.interactively)` ([docs](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/view/scrolldismisseskeyboard(_:))). For buttons, iOS 26 adds `.glass` and `.glassProminent` ([glassProminent](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/primitivebuttonstyle/glassprominent)) and capsule-by-default bordered shapes ([WWDC25 323](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/323/)). The HIG asks for **one or two prominent buttons per view** and a press state on any custom button ([HIG Buttons](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/buttons)). Apple publishes no iPhone button heights, and the docs say `.controlSize(.extraLarge)` "resolves to large" outside visionOS ([ControlSize.extraLarge](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/controlsize/extralarge)). Test before relying on it.

## On iPhone the sidebar collapses into a list, and actions belong in toolbars

The HIG says to prefer split views "in a regular — not a compact — environment" ([HIG Split views](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/split-views)) and to "consider a tab bar first" over a sidebar on iOS ([HIG Sidebars](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/sidebars)). No HIG page says what a sidebar becomes on iPhone; the SwiftUI docs do. `NavigationSplitView` "collapses into a single stack", draws disclosure chevrons on `NavigationLink` rows, and ignores `columnVisibility`. `preferredCompactColumn` picks whether the phone opens on the list or on the detail ([NavigationSplitView](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/navigationsplitview)). The sidebar column therefore becomes the iPhone's root screen, and its New thread, Search, and More rows render as ordinary list rows. That conflicts mildly with two rules: use toolbars, not navigation surfaces, for actions ([HIG Tab bars](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/tab-bars)), and put search at the bottom "if there's room" ([HIG Search fields](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/search-fields)). The "avoid critical actions at the bottom of the sidebar" rule is macOS-only ([HIG Sidebars](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/sidebars)), so the bottom profile button breaks no iOS rule.

Since June 2025 the Toolbars page also covers navigation bars ([HIG Toolbars](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/toolbars)). Its rules for iOS:

- Use a large title to keep people oriented; it collapses on scroll.
- Keep titles concise, "under 15 characters".
- Put only the most important items in the bar, with the rest in a More menu.
- Aim for **at most three groups**.
- Prefer borderless SF Symbols over text, except for actions like Edit.
- Keep text items separate from symbol groups.
- Use the standard Back and Close buttons.
- Have one `.prominent` primary action, on the trailing side.
- Remove custom bar backgrounds.

WWDC25 adds the SwiftUI mechanics: items group automatically on a floating glass surface, `ToolbarSpacer(.fixed/.flexible)` splits groups, and `.sharedBackgroundVisibility(.hidden)` takes an item out of the glass. Apple's example of that last one is a `ProfileButton()` ([WWDC25 323](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/323/); [ToolbarSpacer](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/toolbarspacer)). Apple's Mail-style sample bottom bar runs FilterPicker, flexible spacer, `DefaultToolbarItem(kind: .search)`, fixed spacer, NewMessageButton ([WWDC25 323](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/323/)). `.searchable` applied to the split view moves to the bottom automatically on iPhone, and `.searchToolbarBehavior(.minimize)` turns it into a button ([docs](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/view/searchtoolbarbehavior(_:))). That sample is the clearest primary-source template for a thread list with compose. The HIG has nothing on where an account button goes ([HIG Managing accounts](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/managing-accounts)). A top-bar avatar is convention, not rule.

Menus and sheets complete the picture. Menu labels are verbs in title case with no articles, and take an ellipsis when more input follows. Icons are all-or-none within a group. Submenus go one level deep, and a submenu of more than about five items should become its own menu ([HIG Menus](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/menus)). In iOS 26, icons sit on the leading edge ([WWDC25 323](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/323/)). A pull-down needs at least three items to be "worthwhile". Destructive items go last, render red, and need confirmation ([HIG Pull-down buttons](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/pull-down-buttons)). Context-menu actions must also exist elsewhere in the interface ([HIG Context menus](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/context-menus)). One caveat matters for a custom-font app: forum evidence says SwiftUI `Menu` items ignore the app's font and Dynamic Type overrides and draw in the system menu font ([Forums 709955](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/709955)). No Apple doc confirms this, but the menu's *label* takes Hanken Grotesk and its items will not. Design for that rather than fighting it. Sheets are for brief, scoped tasks, one at a time, with Cancel leading and Done trailing (never Cancel, Done, and Back together). Use a grabber when the sheet resizes, and offer `.medium` on iPhone for progressive disclosure ([HIG Sheets](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/sheets)). In iOS 26, partial sheets are inset glass, so remove `presentationBackground`. A sheet can morph from its toolbar button via `matchedTransitionSource` and `.navigationTransition(.zoom)` ([WWDC25 323](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/323/)).

Several of these pages carry change-log entries dated June 8, 2026 (sidebars, menus, search) and September 9, 2026 (layout), which fall in the WWDC26 cycle. They may describe the next OS more than iOS 26 itself, but nothing in them contradicts the iOS 26 APIs above. One conflict is worth knowing: WWDC25 called the "soft" scroll edge effect the default, while the June 2026 Scroll views page says to prefer `automatic` ([HIG Scroll views](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/scroll-views)). Prefer `automatic`.

## Accessibility sizes demand reflow, labels, and contrast in three appearances

At AX sizes body text runs 28–53 pt. Apple expects layouts to change shape:

- Stack text above secondary items such as glyphs and timestamps.
- Reduce columns.
- Let text wrap to as many lines as needed.
- Show "as much useful text at the largest accessibility font size as you do at the largest standard font size".
- Grow meaningful icons with the text.
- Keep primary elements at the top.

These all come from [HIG Typography](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/typography). Apple's sample switches an `HStack` to a `VStack` at `dynamicTypeSize >= .accessibility1` ([DynamicTypeSize](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/dynamictypesize)). WWDC24 uses `AnyLayout` keyed on `isAccessibilitySize`, warns that fixed frames clip text, and says to "preserve the layout with default or smaller sizes" ([WWDC24 10074](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2024/10074/)). `ViewThatFits` is the content-driven alternative ([docs](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/viewthatfits)). Non-text metrics such as icon frames and padding scale with `@ScaledMetric(relativeTo:)` ([docs](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/scaledmetric)). SF Symbols scale on their own. The Large Content Viewer is only for controls that "must remain small due to unavoidable design constraints", never "as a replacement for proper Dynamic Type support" ([accessibilityShowsLargeContentViewer](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/view/accessibilityshowslargecontentviewer())).

For VoiceOver, the rules are: label every key element, exclude decorative images, describe relationships that are only visual, use headings, and announce content changes ([HIG VoiceOver](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/voiceover)). WWDC24 adds the SwiftUI patterns. `.accessibilityElement(children: .combine)` on a message row merges its labels and turns its sub-buttons into custom actions. A shape such as a `Circle()` gets an accessibility element only once it has a label. `.accessibilityActions {}` exposes hover or overlay controls ([WWDC24 10073](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2024/10073/)). Every gesture also needs an onscreen alternative ([HIG Accessibility](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/accessibility)). Apple's docs don't say whether `swipeActions` reach VoiceOver automatically, so mirror them in a context menu.

Contrast follows WCAG AA as Apple tabulates it: **4.5:1 for text up to 17 pt, and 3:1 for 18 pt and up or bold at any size**. If the default palette falls short, the app must still meet the minimum when Increase Contrast is on, in both light and dark mode ([HIG Accessibility](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/accessibility)). Every custom color needs light, dark, and increased-contrast variants. "Even if your app ships in a single appearance mode", it needs both light and dark so Liquid Glass can adapt ([HIG Color](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/color)). Color must never be the sole carrier of meaning ([HIG Color](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/color)). On glass, put accent color on the *background* of one primary action, not on many glyphs. Apple's 3:1 for small bold text should be treated as a floor rather than a target, and Apple publishes no contrast minimum for non-text icons.

Verification can be largely automated. `XCUIApplication.performAccessibilityAudit(for:_:)` (iOS 17+) runs the Accessibility Inspector audits inside a UI test and fails the test on any issue: element description, hit region, contrast, element detection, clipped text, traits, and Dynamic Type ([docs](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/xcuiautomation/xcuiapplication/performaccessibilityaudit(for:_:)); [WWDC23 10035](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10035/); [Performing accessibility audits](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/accessibility/performing-accessibility-audits-for-your-app)). Its issue handler returns `true` to *ignore* an issue, so filter known false positives by identifier, never by label. In Xcode 26.6, `xcrun simctl ui booted content_size accessibility-extra-extra-extra-large`, `increase_contrast enabled`, and `appearance dark` set the hardest conditions from the command line; this comes from local `simctl` help output, which has no URL. Bold Text, Reduce Transparency, and Reduce Motion have no `simctl` switch and come from Accessibility Inspector's Settings pane ([Testing system accessibility features](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/accessibility/testing-system-accessibility-features-in-your-app)). Apple warns that a clean audit "doesn't guarantee a fully accessible app", so a VoiceOver pass on a device is still required.

## Applying it to b4app: a phone type scale, system-sized rows, one title, a touch composer

**Give iOS its own scale.** The desktop scale (15 pt body, 13 pt rows) sits above macOS's 13 pt default and below iOS's 17 pt, so a single shared number is wrong on at least one platform. `LLAFont` should resolve per platform with `#if os(iOS)`, every iOS role should use `Font.custom(_:size:relativeTo:)` at the HIG Large size, and the `.system(size:)` fallback should go. A role mapping that follows the HIG:

| b4app role | Face | Size at Large | `relativeTo` | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Root list title (nav bar) | system large title | 34 | `.largeTitle` | Standard nav bar renders it |
| Transcript prose, composer text | Hanken Regular | 17 | `.body` | Reaches 48 pt at AX5 (measured) |
| Sidebar/thread row title | Hanken SemiBold or Regular | 17 | `.body` (or `.headline` for SemiBold) | Was 13 pt |
| Row subtitle, preview, speaker label | Hanken Regular | 15 | `.subheadline` | Secondary color, contrast-checked |
| Timestamps, runtime/status metadata | Hanken Regular | 13 | `.footnote` | Not `.caption2` (11→15 jump) |
| Code blocks, tool output | JetBrains Mono Regular (named instance) | 15 | `.body` | Optically equals 17 pt Hanken; wrap or scroll, never truncate |
| Inline code in prose | JetBrains Mono Regular | 15 | `.body` | Same x-height logic |

Map Regular to SemiBold when `legibilityWeight == .bold`. Check the scale at xSmall, Large, xxxLarge, and AX5 using Xcode's Dynamic Type Variants preview ([WWDC24 10074](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2024/10074/)).

**Let rows size themselves, keeping D-053.** New thread, Search, and More stay as rows, as the owner decided. Each row should be a `Label` with an SF Symbol so the icon scales and VoiceOver gets the title. Drop the fixed ~28 pt row height and any `defaultMinListRowHeight` or `listRowInsets` overrides on iOS, so the iOS 26 row height applies and every row clears 44 pt. Use `.contentShape(Rectangle())` so the whole row is tappable. Thread rows should wrap the title, with `lineLimit(1)` only at standard sizes and 2–3 lines at AX sizes. At `isAccessibilitySize` the runtime mark and timestamp should move below the title. Each row should combine into one VoiceOver element, with swipe actions mirrored in a context menu (Rename, Pin, then Delete last, in red). Size the runtime marks and avatars with `@ScaledMetric(relativeTo: .body)` instead of fixed frames. Each mark needs a distinct shape or spoken label (for example `.accessibilityLabel("Claude Code")`), not just a hue, and should be hidden when the row title already names the runtime. Pin the profile button in the bottom safe area with `.safeAreaBar(edge: .bottom)` so the list scrolls under it and it clears the home indicator. Give it a 44 pt hit region, a label naming the account, and a sheet rather than a pull-down if it has fewer than three items (`ProfileMenu.swift`). Its items will render in the system font.

**One title on the thread detail.** Remove the in-content display title and keep the navigation title. The standard choice is a large title on the root list and an inline title on the transcript, as in Messages; the title should be short and truncate rather than wrap. Keep one or two trailing symbol items, with any rarer actions in an ellipsis `Menu`. Don't add a bottom toolbar, because the composer is the bottom chrome.

**A touch composer.** Delete the "Enter sends; Shift-Enter…" hint on iOS. On a phone, Return inserts a newline and the send button sends. Use `TextField("Message", text:, axis: .vertical)` with `.lineLimit(1...6)`. Attach the composer via `.safeAreaBar(edge: .bottom)` and give the transcript `.defaultScrollAnchor(.bottom)` and `.scrollDismissesKeyboard(.interactively)`. Make send the view's only prominent button: a circular `.glassProminent` or `.borderedProminent` button with `arrow.up`, at least 44 pt, labeled "Send", with the cobalt on its background. Keep attach and runtime pickers on the leading edge at 44 pt each. When a turn completes, post an `AccessibilityNotification.Announcement`, not one per token. Under Reduce Motion, make streaming animations fade.

**System margins.** Replace the 24 pt page margins with the defaults: `List` and `ScrollView` margins, `.padding()` without arguments, or `contentMargins`. That puts iPhone gutters at the system's 16–20 pt. On iPad, cap the transcript column with a readable `maxWidth`.

**Gate it.** Add one UI test per screen (sidebar root, thread list, transcript with composer, profile sheet) that calls `performAccessibilityAudit()`. Run it at Large, then again after `simctl ui booted content_size accessibility-extra-extra-extra-large`, then in dark mode with `increase_contrast enabled`. Record the results in `docs/verification.md`.

**Follow-up (HIG-preferred alternative to D-053).** On compact width, move the actions out of the list. Make Search `.searchable` on the split view, which iOS places at the bottom. Put New thread as a trailing `square.and.pencil` in a `.bottomBar` beside it, with an optional filter on the leading side, as in Apple's Mail sample. Make More a trailing ellipsis `Menu` in the nav bar. Put the profile in a top-bar avatar with `.sharedBackgroundVisibility(.hidden)` that morphs into its sheet. This is a design change to D-053 and needs its own decision record.

## Conclusion

The gap between the current iOS build and the HIG is mainly one of *ownership*, not taste. Every number that matters on iPhone is something Apple wants the system to own: row height, margins, bar layout, and how text scales. The desktop scale fails mostly because it pins those numbers. The most useful finding beyond the HIG text is that custom fonts scale on a slightly flatter curve than the system font (48 pt instead of 53 pt for body at AX5). Hanken Grotesk at the HIG base sizes therefore lands a little below SF at the extremes but still above Apple's 200 percent target. The base size, not a multiplier, is the lever to get right.

Two things remain open and deserve measurement rather than assumption: the actual iOS 26 default row height and margin on each iPhone class, and whether SwiftUI swipe actions reach VoiceOver on their own. Both can be read in a simulator in minutes. The June and September 2026 HIG revisions suggest Apple keeps pushing actions toward bottom toolbars and away from in-list rows, which makes the toolbar follow-up to D-053 more likely to look native over time than less.
