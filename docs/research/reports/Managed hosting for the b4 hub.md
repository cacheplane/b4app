# Launch on Fly and Neon, graduate to AWS

Host the hub today as one Fly.io Machine per environment (hub.test.b4.run and hub.b4.run) backed by Neon Postgres, for roughly **$40 a month all-in**, and treat AWS (ECS Express Mode plus Aurora) as the destination for the fail-safe, two-region tier rather than the starting point. Fly is the only managed host in the field that satisfies every hard requirement at the cheapest price: a configurable **`kill_timeout` up to 300 s** covers the 35 s drain, WebSockets and 25 s long-polls survive its 60 s idle timer, a `release_command` runs `b4hub migrate` before traffic moves, secrets are plain env vars, and it is the only candidate whose anycast edge plus `fly-replay` gives a documented path to a second region without Cloudflare ([Fly configuration reference](https://docs.fly.io/reference/configuration); [Fly dynamic request routing](https://docs.fly.io/networking/dynamic-request-routing)). Render is the runner-up (zero-downtime deploys, built-in alerting, $7 per instance) with one caveat the owner must accept: its edge is Cloudflare's network behind the scenes ([Render DDoS protection](https://render.com/docs/ddos-protection)). On the database, Neon Launch wins the "now" tier on price (about $20 to $25 for an always-on 0.25 CU production compute, a few dollars for an autosuspending test compute), 7-day point-in-time restore, a CREATEROLE-capable admin that fits the migrate/serve role split, and copy-on-write branches that make per-PR previews nearly free ([Neon pricing](https://neon.com/pricing); [Neon roles](https://neon.com/docs/manage/roles)); Crunchy Bridge is the runner-up because it is the only non-AWS provider that keeps a cross-region replica path open ([Crunchy Bridge replicas](https://docs.crunchybridge.com/how-to/replicas)). Two findings change the hub's own code rather than the hosting choice: the rate limiter must read a platform-specific client-IP header (on Fly the right-most X-Forwarded-For entry is Fly's own address, not the client), and the service should accept the Apple PEM either base64-encoded or from a `*_FILE` path so the same image runs cleanly on every host and in Compose and Helm ([Fly request headers](https://docs.fly.io/networking/request-headers)). The remaining recommendations, a digest-pinned GitHub Actions pipeline with `test` and `production` Environments, a Compose "production profile" and a Helm chart with a pre-upgrade migration Job, and a $0 observability stack built on Grafana Cloud Free, Sentry Developer and Better Stack, are all achievable before launch by one person.

## Five requirements eliminate half the host field

The brief's constraints are unusually specific for a small service, and they do real work. Heroku's **fixed 30 s SIGTERM window** is below the 35 s drain and cannot be raised, and its Fir generation does not run the `container` stack at all ([Heroku dyno shutdown](https://devcenter.heroku.com/articles/dyno-shutdown-behavior); [Heroku generations](https://devcenter.heroku.com/articles/generations)). AWS App Runner **closed to new customers**, with AWS pointing newcomers at ECS Express Mode, and the Copilot CLI reached end of support in June 2026 ([AWS App Runner availability change](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/apprunner/latest/dg/apprunner-availability-change.html)). Lightsail Containers documents no SIGTERM grace and only a 60 s proxy idle timeout with no official statement on WebSocket upgrades ([Lightsail deployments](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/lightsail/latest/userguide/amazon-lightsail-container-services-deployments.html)). Railway technically qualifies, but its October 2026 breach, in which an attacker ran "an unauthorized query of encrypted environment variables" and decrypted a subset affecting up to about 100,000 workspaces, is the wrong data point for a platform that would hold an Apple private key as an env var; its client-IP header (`X-Real-IP`) has staff-acknowledged bugs, and deploying a prebuilt image from CI requires undocumented GraphQL mutations ([Railway preliminary incident report](https://blog.railway.com/p/preliminary-report-october-security-incident); [Railway Station on client address headers](https://station.railway.com/questions/which-client-address-headers-does-the-ra-fb9e36d7); [Railway Station on CI image deploys](https://station.railway.com/questions/deploy-docker-tag-from-ci-cf2cd5dc)). That leaves Fly.io, Render, DigitalOcean App Platform and ECS on Fargate.

### Fly.io is the primary choice at about $10 a month for both environments

Fly wins on fit, price and future options. Two environments at 1 GB and 512 MB cost **about $10.40 a month** (shared IPv4 is free; a dedicated one is $2) with egress at $0.02/GB in North America and Europe ([Fly pricing](https://docs.fly.io/about/pricing)). The 10 s WebSocket heartbeat keeps connections well inside Fly's 60 s idle rule, which staff describe as "if nothing in or out has been sent or received through them for 60s, then we close them" ([Fly community: request timeouts](https://community.fly.io/t/request-timeouts-on-fly-io/5653)). Secrets "are injected as environment variables into every Machine", `fly secrets set` reads from stdin so the multi-line PEM survives intact, and `--stage` lets CI rotate a secret without an immediate restart ([Fly secrets](https://docs.fly.io/apps/secrets)). The `release_command` runs "in a temporary Machine using the newly built image, once per deploy attempt", does not override the image's `ENTRYPOINT`, and "a non-zero exit status from this command will stop the deployment", so with the hub image's `ENTRYPOINT ["/usr/local/bin/b4hub"]` the configuration is literally `release_command = "migrate"` ([Fly configuration reference](https://docs.fly.io/reference/configuration)). Custom domains from the Vercel zone are a CNAME to the app's `.fly.dev` target plus `fly certs add hub.b4.run`; the first ten single-hostname certificates are free ([Fly custom domains](https://docs.fly.io/networking/custom-domain)). And for the "later" tier, Fly is the only finalist with a built-in primitive for region-aware routing: the `fly-replay: region=` response header replays a request to another region, which is exactly how a replica-region hub would forward writes and stale reads to the primary ([Fly dynamic request routing](https://docs.fly.io/networking/dynamic-request-routing)).

Three things on Fly need deliberate configuration. First, `kill_signal = "SIGTERM"` and `kill_timeout = 60` (35 s drain plus headroom), because the default is SIGINT after 5 s ([Fly configuration reference](https://docs.fly.io/reference/configuration)). Second, the single-instance constraint: a first `fly deploy` creates "two Machines for redundancy" when no process groups are defined, so the app must be scaled to one Machine, and Fly does not document whether a rolling deploy on a single Machine stops the old one before starting the new one ([Fly deploy](https://docs.fly.io/launch/deploy)). The practical answer is to accept a brief overlap or outage window on deploy and document it; because routing state lives in memory, clients already need reconnect-and-resume (see the stepping stones below). Third, the rate limiter: Fly's docs say the right-most X-Forwarded-For address is a shared or dedicated IP assigned to the app and recommend `Fly-Client-IP` when no other proxy sits in front ([Fly request headers](https://docs.fly.io/networking/request-headers)). The hub therefore needs a `CLIENT_IP_SOURCE`-style setting (right-most XFF for ALB and self-hosted proxies, `Fly-Client-IP` on Fly, `do-connecting-ip` on DO) rather than a fixed XFF position.

The honest downsides are operational maturity and the absence of built-in alerting. Fly explicitly "doesn't include built-in alerting on metrics" and its log search retains 7 days ([Fly metrics](https://docs.fly.io/reference/metrics); [Fly logs](https://docs.fly.io/metrics-and-logs/logs)). Aggregators list a roughly 15 h control-plane outage on 5 January 2026 (apps kept running) and several regional Machine boot failures in 2026 ([IsDown Fly.io history](https://isdown.app/status/fly-io/outage-history)). Fly raised a $25M Series D in July 2026 with a CEO change to Scott Johnston and reported its strongest quarter, a smaller raise than Render's $100M extension at a $1.5B valuation in February 2026 ([Fly.io news](https://fly.io/news/fly-io-launches-computers-for-agents.md); [SiliconANGLE on Render](https://siliconangle.com/?p=731299)). For a service whose value is staying connected, control-plane outages matter less than data-plane ones, but the lack of alerting means an external uptime monitor is mandatory from day one, not optional.

### Render is the runner-up at $14 to $39 a month, with a Cloudflare caveat

Render is the better-polished product for a one-person operator: deploys are zero-downtime by default (new instance starts, health checks pass, traffic switches, "Render sends it SIGTERM 60 seconds after the switch, then SIGKILL if it hasn't exited within its shutdown delay"), health-check failures trigger notifications and automatic restarts, and image-backed services redeploy by hitting a deploy hook with `imgURL=<digest>` ([Render deploys](https://render.com/docs/deploys); [Render health checks](https://render.com/docs/health-checks); [Render deploy an image](https://render.com/docs/deploy-an-image)). Two Starter instances cost **$14 a month on the free Hobby workspace**; per-PR previews, dedicated outbound IPs and 14-day log retention need the **$25 flat Pro workspace**, so $39 ([Render workspace plans](https://render.com/changelog/updated-plans-for-render-workspaces); [Render logging](https://render.com/docs/logging)). The pre-deploy command is "available for paid web services", which Starter is ([Render deploys](https://render.com/docs/deploys)). Render loses to Fly on three counts: it always overlaps old and new instances for at least 60 s plus the shutdown delay, which the in-memory routing state tolerates only as a documented window; its X-Forwarded-For handling puts the client first and, per a 2024 user test, does not strip client-supplied entries, so the limiter must trust the first entry rather than the right-most ([Render feedback thread](https://feedback.render.com/features/p/send-the-correct-xforwardedfor)); and its edge is Cloudflare's network, visible in the `_cf-custom-hostname` CNAME its wildcard-domain setup requires ([Render custom domains](https://render.com/docs/custom-domains); [Render DDoS protection](https://render.com/docs/ddos-protection)). If the owner's refusal of Cloudflare is about not contracting with the company, Render's arrangement may be acceptable; if it is about Cloudflare not being in the request path, Render and DigitalOcean are both out and the runner-up becomes ECS Express Mode.

DigitalOcean deserves a mention as the cheapest option with the most built-in alerting: $5 per 512 MiB instance, app-spec `alerts` for CPU, memory, restarts and failed deploys, a `do-connecting-ip` header, `PRE_DEPLOY` jobs and an official GitHub Action with `deploy_pr_preview` ([DO App Spec reference](https://docs.digitalocean.com/products/app-platform/reference/app-spec/); [DO GitHub Actions](https://docs.digitalocean.com/products/app-platform/how-to/deploy-from-github-actions/)). It is third because Cloudflare sits in front of App Platform by default, runtime logs are not retained without forwarding, and, as the database section shows, DigitalOcean's managed Postgres has just lost its cheap HA path ([DO view logs](https://docs.digitalocean.com/products/app-platform/how-to/view-logs/)).

ECS Express Mode is not the "now" choice but it is the "later" one, and it is worth knowing why. Computed from the published rates, one 0.25 vCPU/0.5 GB Fargate task is about $9 a month and the ALB about $16.4 before LCU and IPv4 charges, so two environments sharing one Express Mode ALB land near **$40 a month**, four times Fly ([Fargate pricing](https://aws.amazon.com/fargate/pricing/); [ELB pricing](https://aws.amazon.com/elasticloadbalancing/pricing/); [AWS News Blog on Express Mode](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/aws/build-production-ready-applications-without-infrastructure-complexity-using-amazon-ecs-express-mode)). In exchange ECS is the only platform where exactly-one-instance with no overlap is natively expressible (`minimumHealthyPercent` 0, `maximumPercent` 100), where the ALB in `append` mode makes the right-most X-Forwarded-For entry the correct client address, where the migration task can run under a different task definition whose secrets include only the privileged URL, and where WAF, TLS policy selection, Route 53 health checks and Aurora Global Database are all available in the same account ([ECS service definition parameters](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonECS/latest/developerguide/service_definition_parameters.html); [ALB attributes](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/elasticloadbalancing/latest/application/application-load-balancers.html); [ECS sensitive data](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonECS/latest/developerguide/specifying-sensitive-data.html)). Its `stopTimeout` maximum of 120 s comfortably covers the 35 s drain ([ECS task definition parameters](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonECS/latest/developerguide/task_definition_parameters.html)). The cost of entry is setting up an AWS account, VPC, OIDC trust for GitHub Actions and per-PR preview stacks by hand, which is the work the "now" tier is meant to defer.

### Neon Launch is the primary choice at roughly $25 to $30 for both databases

Neon fits the hub's actual shape. The owner role runs migrations and creates `b4hub_app`; Neon's `neon_superuser` carries CREATEDB and CREATEROLE, which is all that flow needs, and `FORCE ROW LEVEL SECURITY` plus per-transaction `set_config(..., true)` are ordinary table-owner and session operations that no provider restricts ([Neon roles](https://neon.com/docs/manage/roles)). Neon's pooler is PgBouncer in transaction mode with protocol-level prepared statements supported "as of PgBouncer 1.22.0", which is what sqlx uses, and transaction-scoped settings die with the transaction, so the tenant context pattern is safe through the pool ([Neon connection pooling](https://neon.com/docs/connect/connection-pooling)). Pricing is usage-based with no monthly minimum: Launch compute is **$0.106 per CU-hour**, storage **$0.35/GB-month**, instant restore **$0.20/GB-month**, with a 7-day restore window; by arithmetic an always-on 0.25 CU production compute is about $19 a month plus a few dollars of storage, and the test database can autosuspend after five minutes idle on Launch, costing a few dollars a month between test runs ([Neon pricing](https://neon.com/pricing); [Neon plans](https://neon.com/docs/introduction/plans)). Production must have autosuspend disabled (Launch allows this) because Neon's 2026 incident record includes a two-hour period of slow or stuck compute starts for about 1,000 projects in one us-east-1 cell ([IsDown Neon incident](https://isdown.app/status/neon/incidents/584317-database-connectivity-issues-with-projects-in-us-east-1-cell-6)). Branching is the feature that makes Neon the natural partner for Fly previews: branches are copy-on-write, 10 are included, extra branches cost $1.50 per branch-month prorated hourly, branches can carry a TTL for CI, and Neon publishes an end-to-end example that pairs `neondatabase/create-branch-action` with `superfly/fly-pr-review-apps` ([Neon branching](https://neon.com/docs/introduction/branching); [neondatabase/preview-branches-with-fly](https://github.com/neondatabase/preview-branches-with-fly)).

Two Neon caveats must go into the architecture document. First, `neon_superuser` has BYPASSRLS on projects created after August 2023, so migrations must never run as the application role and every RLS test must connect as `b4hub_app`, which is the right discipline anyway ([Neon roles](https://neon.com/docs/manage/roles)). Second, Neon has no cross-region story: "In the event of a region-wide outage, your data is not automatically replicated to another region", and read replicas are same-region computes ([Neon high availability](https://neon.com/docs/introduction/high-availability)). Neon is therefore a "now" and "next" database, not a "later" one, and the plan below treats a database move as part of the later tier. Neon's ownership also changed, with Databricks acquiring it for about $1 billion in May 2025 and compute prices falling 15 to 25 percent afterwards, so far a benefit to customers ([Financier Worldwide](https://financierworldwide.com/fw-news/2025/5/15/databricks-to-acquire-neon-for-1bn); [Vantage on Neon pricing](https://www.vantage.sh/blog/neon-acquisition-new-pricing)).

### Crunchy Bridge is the runner-up because it never closes the multi-region door

Crunchy Bridge costs more at the small end but is the only non-AWS provider that supports the whole roadmap. Physical backups and WAL archiving are included with a **10-day PITR window**, restore is a "Fork from Backup" into a new cluster, and the AWS and Azure marketplace listings describe "superuser access right out of the box" ([Crunchy Bridge backups](https://docs.crunchybridge.com/concepts/backups); [AWS Marketplace listing](https://aws.amazon.com/marketplace/pp/prodview-orfodimax3czu)). Crucially, Crunchy lets you "provision read replicas across regions and infrastructure provider" and detach a replica into a standalone primary, which is a workable manual region-failover primitive ([Crunchy Bridge replicas](https://docs.crunchybridge.com/how-to/replicas)). The cost of a credible production footprint is Hobby-0 at $9 for test plus Standard-4 at $70 for production (Hobby plans are "not intended for production usage" and cannot have HA), so about **$79 a month**, rising to about $149 when the same-size HA standby is added ([Crunchy Bridge plans and pricing](https://docs.crunchybridge.com/concepts/plans-pricing); [Crunchy Bridge HA](https://docs.crunchybridge.com/concepts/high-availability)). A review site mentions a Snowflake acquisition of Crunchy Data; no primary source confirmed it in this research, so treat vendor stability as an open question to check before committing ([rfp.wiki](https://www.rfp.wiki/vendors/crunchy-data)).

Fly Managed Postgres is the tempting third option because it lives inside the Fly private network with no egress and includes HA, backups and PgBouncer on every plan from **$38** ([Fly pricing](https://fly.io/pricing)). It is not recommended until a trial confirms that its fixed `schema_admin`/`writer`/`reader` role set can express "migration role creates and grants to `b4hub_app`", because `fly mpg users set-role` offers only those three and a Fly staffer said superuser "isn't something they're planning at this time" ([fly mpg users set-role](https://fly.io/docs/flyctl/mpg-users-set-role/); [Fly community on MPG superuser](https://community.fly.io/t/mpg-superuser-permissions-for-extensions/28124)). It also has no in-place major upgrade, no published PITR window, and "no alerts for CPU or storage" ([Fly MPG overview](https://docs.fly.io/mpg/overview)). DigitalOcean's $15 plan is the cheapest with included 7-day PITR, but from 15 October 2026 new Standard Edition clusters "cannot use plans with more than 4 GiB of RAM or add standby or read-only nodes", so the HA step jumps to Advanced Edition at $130 plus a $130 standby ([DO PostgreSQL pricing](https://docs.digitalocean.com/products/databases/postgresql/details/pricing/)). Supabase's $100-a-month PITR add-on roughly quintuples the small-plan cost and its shared pooler does not support prepared statements in transaction mode, which rules it out for a sqlx service ([Supabase backups](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/platform/backups); [Supabase connecting](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/database/connecting-to-postgres)).

Whichever provider is chosen, two design rules follow from the pooling research. A LISTEN/NOTIFY worker needs one direct, non-pooled connection per hub instance because every built-in pooler defaults to transaction mode, where LISTEN silently lands on a backend that returns to the pool ([Neon connection pooling](https://neon.com/docs/connect/connection-pooling); [PgBouncer issue #655](https://github.com/pgbouncer/pgbouncer/issues/655)). And 25 s long-poll readers must wait on an in-process broadcast fed by that single LISTEN connection rather than hold a pooled connection each, or the small direct-connection limits (Neon 104 at 0.25 CU, DigitalOcean 22 at 1 GiB) run out quickly ([Neon connection pooling](https://neon.com/docs/connect/connection-pooling); [DO PostgreSQL limits](https://docs.digitalocean.com/products/databases/postgresql/details/limits/)).

## One GitHub Actions pipeline ships both environments by digest

The release model in the brief maps onto GitHub primitives cleanly, and the repo already has the right image shape: `Hub/Dockerfile` is a two-stage build with digest-pinned bases, `ENTRYPOINT ["/usr/local/bin/b4hub"]`, `CMD ["serve"]`, a `HEALTHCHECK` on `/readyz`, and a separate `migrate` subcommand backed by sqlx 0.8.6. The build job should add cargo-chef layers (planner, cook, build) so dependency compilation is cached, use `docker/build-push-action@v7` with `cache-to: type=gha,mode=max`, and build arm64 natively on GitHub's free `ubuntu-24.04-arm` runners for public repositories rather than under QEMU, merging the two digests into one index with `docker buildx imagetools create` ([cargo-chef](https://github.com/LukeMathWalker/cargo-chef); [Docker GitHub Actions cache](https://docs.docker.com/build/ci/github-actions/cache/); [GitHub changelog on arm64 runners](https://github.blog/changelog/2025-08-07-arm64-hosted-runners-for-public-repositories-are-now-generally-available/)). Because `cacheplane/b4app` is public, provenance attestations at `mode=max` are added automatically; adding `sbom: true` and `actions/attest@v4` with `push-to-registry: true` gives SLSA Build Level 2 and lets the deploy job gate on `gh attestation verify oci://ghcr.io/cacheplane/b4hub@<digest> -R cacheplane/b4app`, the only enforcement point available since none of Fly, Render or ECS verifies signatures at deploy time ([Docker build attestations in GitHub Actions](https://docs.docker.com/build/ci/github-actions/attestations/); [GitHub artifact attestations](https://docs.github.com/en/actions/security-for-github-actions/using-artifact-attestations/using-artifact-attestations-to-establish-provenance-for-builds)). Public GHCR packages also avoid the private-registry friction on every host: Fly's documented path for private external registries is to re-push to `registry.fly.io`, and Railway requires Pro for private pulls ([Fly Docker registry blueprint](https://docs.fly.io/blueprints/using-the-fly-docker-registry); [Railway services guide](https://docs.railway.com/guides/services#deploying-a-public-docker-image)).

Tagging must coexist with the existing `mac-v<version>` releases. Use Git tags `hub-v1.4.0`, which `docker/metadata-action` maps to image tags `1.4.0`, `1.4`, `1` and `latest` via `type=match,pattern=hub-v(.*),group=1` (or `type=semver` with `match=`), plus `sha-<long sha>` on every build, `main` on branch pushes and `nightly`/`nightly-YYYYMMDD` on schedule; this is the same `<component>-v<version>` convention release-please uses for monorepos ([docker/metadata-action](https://github.com/docker/metadata-action); [release-please manifest releaser](https://github.com/googleapis/release-please/blob/main/docs/manifest-releaser.md)). Every deploy, including rollbacks, should consume the immutable digest from `steps.build.outputs.digest`, never a tag, so test, production and rollback refer to the identical artifact; Render documents this preference explicitly ("A digest always refers to the same image, while a tag may now point to a different one") ([Render rollbacks](https://render.com/docs/rollbacks)).

Three GitHub Environments carry the policy. `test` deploys on every push to `main` with `concurrency: { group: deploy-test }`. `production` deploys on `release: types: [published]` guarded by `if: startsWith(github.event.release.tag_name, 'hub-v')` (the `mac-v*` releases fire the same event and are filtered out), with a deployment tag rule `hub-v*`, required reviewers if wanted (one approval suffices, "Prevent self-review" available), and `concurrency: { group: deploy-production, queue: max }` so every release deploys in order; never set `cancel-in-progress: true` on a job that runs migrations ([GitHub managing environments](https://docs.github.com/en/actions/managing-workflow-runs-and-deployments/managing-deployments/managing-environments-for-deployment); [GitHub events](https://docs.github.com/en/actions/writing-workflows/choosing-when-your-workflow-runs/events-that-trigger-workflows); [GitHub concurrency](https://docs.github.com/en/actions/writing-workflows/choosing-what-your-workflow-does/control-the-concurrency-of-workflows-and-jobs)). Environments with required reviewers are free on public repositories. Per-PR previews run only for same-repo PRs (`if: github.event.pull_request.head.repo.full_name == github.repository`) because fork PRs cannot read `FLY_API_TOKEN` or `NEON_API_KEY`; the job creates a Neon branch, runs `migrate` against the privileged branch URL, and passes the app-role URL to `superfly/fly-pr-review-apps` as a secret, with the branch deleted on close ([superfly/fly-pr-review-apps](https://github.com/superfly/fly-pr-review-apps); [Neon GitHub integration](https://neon.com/docs/guides/neon-github-integration)). Nightly builds use `schedule: cron '17 3 * * *'` (offset from the hour, UTC, default branch only), skip when `HEAD` matches the last nightly's recorded SHA unless that run failed, and must use a non-`hub-v` tag if attached to a prerelease so the production job does not fire ([GitHub events](https://docs.github.com/en/actions/writing-workflows/choosing-when-your-workflow-runs/events-that-trigger-workflows)). GHCR has no retention policy, so prune with `dataaxiom/ghcr-cleanup-action`, which handles multi-arch indexes and attestation referrers that naive "delete untagged" rules would orphan ([dataaxiom/ghcr-cleanup-action](https://github.com/dataaxiom/ghcr-cleanup-action)).

Migrations run before traffic switches on every host and must be forward-only. On Fly the `release_command` Machine sees the app's full secret set, so the privileged `MIGRATE_DATABASE_URL` would also be present in the serving Machine's environment; the mitigation is either to have `serve` refuse to start if the privileged variable is set, or to run `docker run ghcr.io/cacheplane/b4hub@<digest> migrate` from the Actions job itself against the allow-listed database before calling `fly deploy`, keeping the privileged URL in a GitHub Environment secret only ([Fly configuration reference](https://docs.fly.io/reference/configuration)). Because old `serve` instances briefly run against the new schema during the overlap, every migration must be backward-compatible (expand/contract), and sqlx reversible migrations require `-r` on the first migration ever created, so the realistic stance is forward-only with application rollback by redeploying the previous digest ([sqlx-cli README](https://github.com/launchbadge/sqlx/blob/main/sqlx-cli/README.md)).

## Now, next, later: from one Machine to a drilled two-region failover

The stepping stones below are chosen so that each stage's work is not thrown away by the next, and so that the hub's own invariant, a server-assigned monotonic per-tenant sequence, does the correctness work at every stage.

| Stage | Hub topology | Database | Rough monthly cost | What the hub code must do |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Now (launch) | 1 Fly Machine per environment, same region (iad or ewr), `kill_signal` SIGTERM, `kill_timeout` 60 | Neon Launch: prod always-on 0.25 CU, test autosuspends | Fly ~$10, Neon ~$25 to $30, observability $0, total ~$40 | Platform-selectable client-IP header; PEM via base64 or `*_FILE`; on SIGTERM fail `/readyz`, close WebSockets with code 1001, return long-polls early; clients reconnect and resume from cursor |
| Next (resilient in one region) | 2 Machines, `bluegreen` deploys with health checks | Neon with restore drills, or move to Crunchy Standard-4 + HA (~$149) or RDS Multi-AZ (~$23 instance, third-party price) | $70 to $150 for the database, ~$20 for compute | Move routing state out of process memory: NOTIFY as a wake-up hint carrying `{tenant_id, seq}` over a dedicated LISTEN connection, change log as source of truth; pool `test_before_acquire`, bounded `max_lifetime`, reconnect loop on the LISTEN connection |
| Later (multi-region, single writer) | Primary region plus replica region; either Fly anycast + `fly-replay region=` or ECS Express in two AWS regions behind Route 53 health checks or Global Accelerator | Aurora Global Database (planned switchover RPO 0, under 30 s; unplanned RTO minutes) or Crunchy cross-region replica with detach | $300 to $1,000+ before cross-region transfer | Replica-region hub answers a subscription only if `max(seq)` for the tenant on the replica is at or past the client cursor, else forwards to primary; human-initiated promotion runbook, drilled quarterly |

Sources: [Fly pricing](https://docs.fly.io/about/pricing), [Neon pricing](https://neon.com/pricing), [Crunchy Bridge plans and pricing](https://docs.crunchybridge.com/concepts/plans-pricing), [Bytebase db.t4g.micro](https://www.bytebase.com/dbcost/rds/instance/db.t4g.micro), [Aurora Global Database](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonRDS/latest/AuroraUserGuide/aurora-global-database-disaster-recovery.html), [Fly dynamic request routing](https://docs.fly.io/networking/dynamic-request-routing), [Route 53 health checks](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/Route53/latest/DeveloperGuide/dns-failover.html), [Global Accelerator](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/global-accelerator/latest/dg/introduction-how-it-works.html).

The "now" tier is deliberately single-instance, and the only thing that makes that safe is the client. No managed host keeps an existing WebSocket alive across a deploy; Render says so plainly ("When the old instance eventually terminates, active WebSocket connections break"), and Fly's `kill_timeout` is "best-effort" ([Render zero-downtime deploys](https://render.com/articles/how-render-handles-zero-downtime-deploys); [Fly configuration reference](https://docs.fly.io/reference/configuration/)). Reconnect with jittered backoff and resume from the per-tenant cursor makes deploy-induced disconnects invisible to the sync result; only latency suffers. Backups at this tier should include a nightly `pg_dump` to an owner-controlled bucket in a second region, because no provider page read in this research commits to where backups are stored relative to the primary region, and because the hub's data model (every device holds a full local copy) means a restore can detect that its max sequence is behind a device and re-ingest the tail ([Pigsty PITR drill](https://doc.pigsty.io/docs/pgsql/tutorial/pitr/)).

The "next" tier is where the architecture earns its multi-instance capability, and PostgreSQL LISTEN/NOTIFY is the right first fan-out bus for this workload. The widely cited 2025 failure at Recall.ai came from a global `AccessExclusiveLock` taken at commit by any transaction that issued NOTIFY under "tens of thousands of simultaneous writers"; the hub's writes are small, infrequent and per-tenant, so the lock will not bite, and the two real hazards (transaction-pooled LISTEN and missed notifications during reconnect) are solved by the dedicated connection and by treating NOTIFY as a hint to re-query the change log from the cursor ([Recall.ai](https://recall.ai/blog/postgres-listen-notify-does-not-scale); [PostgreSQL NOTIFY docs](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/sql-notify.html)). Redis Pub/Sub and NATS Core have the same at-most-once semantics and would add a second stateful service without improving correctness. Fly's `fly-replay instance=` device affinity is the alternative that keeps routing in memory, but it locks the design to Fly, is reported flaky in community threads, and does not survive the pinned Machine being replaced in a deploy ([Fly community on instance replay](https://community.fly.io/t/fly-replay-to-specific-instance-failing-despite-machine-being-started-and-responsive/26928)). On the database side, only AWS publishes failover figures: RDS Multi-AZ with two readable standbys fails over "typically under 35 seconds" with synchronous replication, while Crunchy says "in an instant" and Render "a few seconds" (asynchronous, so recent writes can be lost) without numbers, and Neon has no standby at all ([AWS RDS Multi-AZ](https://aws.amazon.com/rds/features/multi-az); [Crunchy Bridge HA](https://docs.crunchybridge.com/concepts/high-availability); [Render HA](https://render.com/docs/postgresql-high-availability); [Neon high availability](https://neon.com/docs/introduction/high-availability)). If the change log must never lose a row the hub acknowledged, the "next" database should be synchronous (RDS Multi-AZ, or Crunchy after confirming its replication mode with support), and the hub should treat a post-failover sequence regression as a hard error that re-requests the lost tail from devices.

The "later" tier should be a manually promoted warm standby in a second region with a drilled runbook, not automatic cross-region database failover. AWS warns that unplanned Aurora Global failover "doesn't wait for data to synchronize" and that write fencing is best-effort so "writes might be momentarily accepted in the old primary Region, causing split-brain issues", while planned switchover gives RPO 0 and under 30 s on recent engine versions; a Fly staffer put the general view bluntly: "Automatic failover between regions is risky, and not something Postgres is designed for" ([Aurora Global Database](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonRDS/latest/AuroraUserGuide/aurora-global-database-disaster-recovery.html); [Fly community on multi-region Postgres](https://community.fly.io/t/postgres-high-availability-single-vs-multi-region-leader-replica/4601/2)). The research found no first-hand account of a small team completing a multi-region Postgres move in 2024 to 2026, which is itself a signal to keep this stage simple. Two app-layer routing stories exist without Cloudflare: Fly's anycast with `fly-replay`, and AWS Route 53 health checks or Global Accelerator (two static anycast IPs, 340 s TCP idle, so WebSockets must send a byte within that window) ([Global Accelerator](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/global-accelerator/latest/dg/introduction-how-it-works.html)). Given that AWS is permitted and is the only vendor with published RTO/RPO numbers, WAF, and a managed global database, the recommended end state is ECS Express Mode in two regions with Aurora Global Database; the alternative that avoids an AWS migration is Fly in two regions with a Crunchy Bridge cross-region replica. Multi-writer Postgres (pgEdge Spock, EDB PGD) is the wrong fit because its Snowflake-style global sequences are unique but not dense or ordered, which breaks the "never miss a change it was told exists" invariant ([pgEdge Spock](https://github.com/pgEdge/spock); [EDB PGD conflicts](https://www.enterprisedb.com/docs/pgd/latest/conflict-management/conflicts/00_conflicts_overview/)).

## The same image ships as Compose and a Helm chart

The on-prem packaging should preserve the migrate/serve privilege split that the hosted deployment uses, which makes it stricter than the typical self-hosted product but costs nothing extra. For Docker Compose, the production profile is three services: `db` on the official `postgres:17` image with a named volume, a `pg_isready` health check and `restart: unless-stopped`; `migrate` using the hub image with `command: [migrate]`, `restart: "no"`, `depends_on: db: condition: service_healthy` and the privileged URL; and `hub` with `command: [serve]`, `depends_on: migrate: condition: service_completed_successfully`, `init: true`, `stop_grace_period: 40s` and the application-role URL. The Compose spec supports this directly: `service_completed_successfully` "specifies that a dependency is expected to run to successful completion before starting a dependent service" ([Compose services reference](https://docs.docker.com/reference/compose-file/services/)). Plausible, Umami and Infisical all migrate in-process at app start instead, so this design is a deliberate departure ([Plausible compose](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/plausible/community-edition/v3.2.1/compose.yml); [Infisical Compose](https://www.infisical.com/docs/self-hosting/deployment-options/docker-compose)). Pin the Postgres major because, as Gitea's docs put it, "PostgreSQL does not upgrade its data directory on its own" ([Gitea install with Docker](https://docs.gitea.com/installation/install-with-docker)). TLS is an optional `caddy:2` profile with `reverse_proxy hub:8080`, since Caddy's automatic HTTPS needs only DNS pointing at the host, ports 80 and 443, and persistent storage ([Caddy automatic HTTPS](https://caddyserver.com/docs/automatic-https)). Secrets go in Compose `secrets:` with `file:` sources mounted at `/run/secrets/<name>`, which is why the hub should grow `*_FILE` variants of its PEM and database-URL variables; the upgrade runbook is `docker compose pull && docker compose run --rm migrate && docker compose up -d`, and backup is a scheduled `pg_dump -Fc` plus the PEM files.

The Helm chart should start from the `helm create` skeleton, depend on no library chart, and require an external PostgreSQL. Helm 4 shipped in November 2025 with the chart format unchanged, and Helm 3 reaches security end of life on 11 November 2026, so documentation should target Helm 4 and require at least 3.17 ([Helm 4 released](https://helm.sh/blog/helm-4-released)). Bitnami is the reason not to bundle Postgres or use `bitnami/common`: since 28 August 2025 "no new Bitnami container images or Helm charts have been published to Docker Hub", versioned images moved to a frozen `bitnamilegacy` archive, and projects like Metacat dropped their Postgres subchart in favour of "bring your own database" with a CloudNativePG recipe ([bitnami/charts issue #35164](https://github.com/bitnami/charts/issues/35164); [Metacat chart 3.0.0](https://github.com/NCEAS/metacat/releases/tag/chart-3.0.0)). The chart should ship an `examples/cnpg-cluster.yaml` and a `database.existingSecret` contract instead. Migrations are a Kubernetes Job with `helm.sh/hook: pre-install,pre-upgrade`, `hook-weight: "-5"`, `hook-delete-policy: before-hook-creation,hook-succeeded`, `backoffLimit: 0`, `activeDeadlineSeconds` around 600 and `ttlSecondsAfterFinished` as a fallback because "resources created by hooks are not tracked as part of the release"; "if a hook fails, the release fails" before the Deployment is touched, which is exactly the semantics the hosted `release_command` gives ([Helm chart hooks](https://helm.sh/docs/topics/charts_hooks/)). Sentry's chart is the closest precedent, with hook-wrapped migration Jobs and a separate hook Secret ([Sentry chart README](https://github.com/sentry-kubernetes/charts/blob/develop/charts/sentry/README.md)). The privileged URL lives in its own Secret that only the hook Job mounts.

Networking and lifecycle defaults follow from the hub's shape. Because ingress-nginx was retired in March 2026 with the recommendation to "begin migration to Gateway API or another Ingress controller immediately", the chart should render a controller-agnostic `Ingress` with free-form annotations and an optional Gateway API `HTTPRoute` whose `timeouts` stanza (Standard channel since v1.2) defaults to disabled for long-lived WebSockets ([Kubernetes blog: ingress-nginx retirement](https://kubernetes.io/blog/2025/11/11/ingress-nginx-retirement/); [Kubernetes blog: Gateway API v1.2](https://v1-35.docs.kubernetes.io/blog/2024/11/21/gateway-api-v1-2/)). cert-manager handles Gateway listeners in beta without a feature gate since 1.15 ([cert-manager Gateway usage](https://cert-manager.io/docs/usage/gateway/)). Set `terminationGracePeriodSeconds: 40` with a short `preStop` sleep so endpoint removal propagates before SIGTERM (the Kubernetes default is 30 s and the `preStop` hook counts against the grace period), use `strategy: Recreate` while `replicaCount` is 1 and routing state is in memory, leave the PodDisruptionBudget off by default because with one replica a percentage budget still allows 100 percent unavailability, and ship Restricted Pod Security Standard defaults: `runAsNonRoot`, a non-zero UID such as 10001, `allowPrivilegeEscalation: false`, `capabilities.drop: [ALL]`, `seccompProfile.type: RuntimeDefault`, plus `readOnlyRootFilesystem: true` ([Kubernetes Pod lifecycle](https://kubernetes.io/docs/concepts/workloads/pods/pod-lifecycle/); [Kubernetes PDB](https://kubernetes.io/docs/tasks/run-application/configure-pdb/); [Kubernetes Pod Security Standards](https://kubernetes.io/docs/concepts/security/pod-security-standards/)). Multi-line PEM goes in Secret `stringData`, preferably mounted as a file, with `--set-file` documented as the CLI path so a key never lands in a committed values file ([Kubernetes Secrets](https://kubernetes.io/docs/concepts/configuration/secret/)). Observability passes through the standard `OTEL_EXPORTER_OTLP_*` variables and renders a `ServiceMonitor` only when the `monitoring.coreos.com/v1` API exists ([OTLP exporter configuration](https://opentelemetry.io/docs/specs/otel/protocol/exporter/)). Distribute the chart as an OCI artifact at `oci://ghcr.io/cacheplane/charts/b4hub`, cosign-sign the pushed digest keylessly from the same release workflow, generate the README with helm-docs, lint with `ct lint` and `helm lint --strict`, unit-test with helm-unittest, validate with kubeconform, ship `values.schema.json`, and make every image reference overridable via `global.imageRegistry` for air-gapped installs ([Helm registries](https://helm.sh/docs/topics/registries/); [Artifact Hub OCI charts](https://artifacthub.io/docs/topics/repositories/helm-charts/); [helm-docs](https://github.com/norwoodj/helm-docs)).

## A $0 observability stack and in-app edge controls are enough at launch

Every host keeps stdout logs for days, not months: Fly's app log search retains 7 days, Render 7 days on Hobby, Railway 7 days on Hobby, and DigitalOcean does not retain runtime logs without forwarding ([Fly logs](https://docs.fly.io/metrics-and-logs/logs); [Render logging](https://render.com/docs/logging); [Railway logging](https://docs.railway.com/reference/logging)). The only export path that works identically everywhere is OTLP from the Rust process itself, because Railway has no log drain and Fly's shipper is a separate app to run. The opentelemetry-rust crates are at 0.33.x with Logs and Metrics API, SDK and OTLP exporters stable and "Distributed Tracing remains pre-stable"; pair `opentelemetry` 0.33 with `tracing-opentelemetry` 0.34, emit one JSON object per line from `tracing_subscriber::fmt().json()` to stdout at INFO, wrap routes in tower-http `TraceLayer`, add sqlx spans by hand with `#[instrument]`, and head-sample traces at 10 to 20 percent with 100 percent of errors ([opentelemetry-rust releases](https://github.com/open-telemetry/opentelemetry-rust/releases); [tracing-opentelemetry docs](https://docs.rs/tracing-opentelemetry/latest/tracing_opentelemetry/)). Grafana Cloud Free takes all three signals (10k active series, 50 GB logs, 50 GB traces, 14-day retention, 3 users) and bundles synthetics and IRM on-call paging for 3 users with phone and SMS; Honeycomb Free accepts 20M events a month over native OTLP; Sentry Developer is free forever with 5k errors and 5M spans and has a `tracing` layer for Rust ([Grafana Cloud pricing](https://grafana.com/pricing/); [Honeycomb pricing](https://www.honeycomb.io/pricing); [Sentry pricing](https://sentry.io/pricing/); [Sentry Rust tracing](https://docs.sentry.io/platforms/rust/guides/tracing/)). Datadog is the cost warning: no durable free tier, roughly $15 to $36 per host plus log fees ([Datadog pricing](https://www.datadoghq.com/pricing/)). The realistic launch stack is Grafana Cloud Free for metrics, traces, synthetics and paging, Sentry Developer for panics and new error classes, the host's log viewer for live debugging, and optionally Axiom Personal for 30-day log retention, at **$0 a month**, with Grafana Pro at $19 or Sentry Team at $29 as the first paid step.

Uptime monitoring and paging cost nothing and are mandatory on Fly. Better Stack Free offers 10 monitors at 3-minute checks and one status page; UptimeRobot Free offers 50 monitors at 5 minutes; Checkly Hobby offers 10 monitors at 2 minutes and is the only one that documents a scripted `wss://` check ([Better Stack pricing](https://betterstack.com/pricing); [UptimeRobot pricing](https://uptimerobot.com/pricing/); [Checkly pricing](https://www.checklyhq.com/pricing/); [Checkly WebSocket checks](https://checklyhq.com/docs/multistep-checks/websockets/)). The cheapest reliable WebSocket probe is to have `/readyz` open a loopback WebSocket internally and report the result, so any HTTP monitor covers the WebSocket path. Route alerts through one tool that escalates from push to phone call (Better Stack voice alerts or Grafana IRM), subscribe to the Fly and Neon status pages, keep the runbook in `docs/`, and put a monthly calendar reminder to restore the latest backup into a scratch database and run `migrate` and `/readyz` against it. A public status page is free at every uptime vendor and is worth wiring to the `/readyz` monitor at launch, if only to have somewhere to point support replies.

Edge security on Fly, Render and Railway is the application's job, because none offers an always-on, user-configurable WAF or rate limiter. Fly's TLS terminates at Fly Proxy with `tls_options.versions = ["TLSv1.2", "TLSv1.3"]` configurable and `http_options.response.headers` able to inject HSTS, and it has infrastructure-level DDoS protection with no WAF; Render's DDoS protection covers L3/L4 floods but its own article notes that L7 attacks resembling real traffic can still reach the app; Railway's "Under Attack Mode" blocks all non-browser traffic and so cannot be used for an API ([Fly TLS termination](https://docs.fly.io/security/tls-termination); [Render DDoS article](https://render.com/articles/how-render-handles-ddos-attacks); [Railway WAF](https://docs.railway.com/networking/waf)). The launch posture is therefore in-app: `tower_governor` with one shared `GovernorConfig` per route group and a background `retain_recent()` task, keyed on the platform's client-IP header, with tight limits on the Apple callback and WebAuthn routes (on the order of 5 per minute per IP with burst 10), looser limits on token refresh and WebSocket upgrades, and a per-account limiter after authentication; `RequestBodyLimitLayer` returning 413 (64 KiB on auth routes, 1 MiB default); `Strict-Transport-Security` with `includeSubDomains; preload`, `X-Content-Type-Options: nosniff`, `Referrer-Policy: no-referrer` and `Cache-Control: no-store` on auth responses ([tower_governor](https://docs.rs/tower_governor); [tower_http limit](https://docs.rs/tower-http/latest/tower_http/limit/index.html)). AWS WAF becomes available only at the ECS tier, where a web ACL, the Common Rule Set and a rate-based rule cost about $8 a month at 1M requests and Bot Control adds $10 ([AWS WAF pricing](https://aws.amazon.com/waf/pricing/)).

The Apple callback needs specific handling. Apple's `form_post` response mode delivers the result as a cross-site navigation POST from `https://appleid.apple.com` to the HTTPS `redirect_uri`; no CORS preflight is involved, but any state cookie must be `SameSite=None; Secure; HttpOnly`, scoped to the callback path and short-lived (about ten minutes), because `SameSite=Lax` cookies are not sent on a cross-site POST ([Apple Sign in with Apple REST API](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/signinwithapplerestapi/request-an-authorization-to-the-sign-in-with-apple-server.); [Arctic Apple provider](https://v1.arcticjs.dev/providers/apple)). Keep CORS closed for the rest of the API (the clients are native apps), exempt only the callback route from CSRF checks by validating `state` against that cookie, respond with a `303` to the custom scheme carrying a one-time code rather than the identity token, add `Content-Security-Policy: frame-ancestors 'none'` to the fallback HTML, and never log the code.

Secret hygiene in logs is the last launch item and the one most likely to be forgotten. sqlx logs every executed statement at DEBUG by default and statements slower than one second at WARN, so a `RUST_LOG=debug` deployment prints SQL; call `disable_statement_logging()` or `log_statements(LevelFilter::Trace)` on `PgConnectOptions` so a misconfigured env var cannot re-enable it, and set production `RUST_LOG` to `info,sqlx=warn` ([sqlx ConnectOptions](https://docs.rs/sqlx/latest/sqlx/trait.ConnectOptions.html)). Place `SetSensitiveRequestHeadersLayer` for `Authorization` and `Cookie` before `TraceLayer` so `HeaderValue::set_sensitive` redacts Debug output ([tower_http sensitive_headers](https://docs.rs/tower-http/latest/tower_http/sensitive_headers/index.html)). Wrap Apple identity tokens, WebAuthn challenges and device bearer tokens in a `Secret<String>` newtype whose `Debug` prints a fixed marker, log only a SHA-256 fingerprint when correlation is needed, and add an integration test that drives a login path with a known token through a JSON subscriber writing to memory and asserts the token never appears, including at `RUST_LOG=trace`. Dependency hygiene is `cargo-deny` on PRs and weekly, Dependabot for `cargo`, `docker` (version updates only; it does no Docker security updates) and `github-actions`, and `aquasecurity/trivy-action` scanning the built image with SARIF upload, since GHCR documents no vulnerability scanning of its own ([GitHub Dependabot ecosystems](https://docs.github.com/en/code-security/dependabot/ecosystems-supported-by-dependabot/supported-ecosystems-and-repositories); [aquasecurity/trivy-action](https://github.com/aquasecurity/trivy-action)).

## Conclusion

The most useful result of this research is that the hosting decision is smaller than it looks and the code decisions are larger. Fly plus Neon at about $40 a month is a defensible launch, but the choice is reversible precisely because the recommended pipeline deploys an immutable GHCR digest with a `migrate` step the image already carries, the configuration is plain env vars, and the schema is portable SQL; moving to Render, DigitalOcean or ECS Express later is a workflow change, not a rewrite. What is not reversible cheaply is the set of assumptions baked into the hub: that the client IP is the right-most X-Forwarded-For entry (true on ALB and self-hosted proxies, false on Fly, Render and DigitalOcean), that a WebSocket survives a deploy (false everywhere), and that one instance owns routing state (which blocks the second Machine). Making the IP source configurable, making clients resume from their cursor, and planning the NOTIFY-hint fan-out now are the three changes that turn the "now" tier into a stepping stone rather than a dead end.

The second insight is that "fail-safe" for this hub is cheaper than for a typical SaaS because every device holds a full copy of its tenant's log and the server assigns a gapless per-tenant sequence. That invariant lets a restored or promoted database detect that it is behind a device and re-ingest the tail, lets a replica-region hub decide locally whether it may answer a subscription, and makes multi-writer Postgres unnecessary. The realistic fail-safe end state is a drilled, human-initiated promotion of a warm standby in a second AWS region, not automatic cross-region failover, and the decisions that keep that door open are to prefer synchronous replication when HA is added, to keep a nightly dump in an owner-controlled bucket from day one, and to leave Neon for Aurora or Crunchy at the "next" tier rather than discovering at the "later" tier that the database cannot leave its region.
